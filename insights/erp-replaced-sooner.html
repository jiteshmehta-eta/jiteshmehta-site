Your ERP Will Be Replaced Sooner Than Gartner Thinks

For boards, CEOs and CIOs: why AI and agentic AI are taking the big ERP suites apart one process at a time, starting in midsize companies, and what to do before the 2027 support deadline.

By Jitesh Mehta, Independent Advisor · November 2026

In 1997, I was on the SAP R/3 implementation team at Crompton Greaves, moving a power transformer factory off Excel purchase orders and manual registers. Years later at BG, I lived through the move from Maximo to SAP. I have seen ERP arrive twice. I now expect to see it leave, and not in the way most people imagine.

On 10 November 2026, CIO reported that IT leaders are now listing SAP's ERP suites among the software they may replace, alongside VMware and Oracle's Java. The reasons given were rising licence costs, the end of vendor support, and AI assisted coding that makes building in-house alternatives more feasible. Gartner research puts about 22 percent of IT leaders as actively reconsidering core systems such as ERP and CRM.

Gartner also told CIO that IT leaders are not yet planning wholesale replacement of core systems, and that institutional trust, regulatory barriers and data lock-in will keep most CIOs from major replacements for the next three to six years.

Gartner is right about the big bang. It is wrong about the timing, because the replacement is not coming as a big bang.

It is coming one process at a time. An approval workflow here, a maintenance notification app there, an agent that reads supplier invoices and matches them before a person opens the inbox. Each one is small. Each one removes a reason to pay for a module. ERP will not be switched off. It will be hollowed out. The driving force is AI, and above all agentic AI. Seven forces are pushing it.

1. The licence bill keeps rising while the value does not

SAP is moving customers off perpetually licensed ECC and onto subscription based S/4HANA. A perpetual licence was paid once. A subscription is paid every year, for as long as you run the system. I have called this buying a 100 tonne crane to lift 40 tonne loads. Under a subscription, you rent that crane forever. Every board that sees the renewal will ask what it is actually lifting.

2. The support clock forces the decision

SAP's maintenance timeline for ECC is now fixed in stages:

End 2025Enhancement packages 0 to 5 leave mainstream maintenance
End 2027Mainstream maintenance ends for ECC 6.0 on packages 6 to 8
2028 to 2030Extended maintenance, at two extra percentage points
2031 to 2033Transition option, only inside RISE with SAP
After 2033RISE or GROW with SAP

The ECC support clock

A deadline forces every board to put a price on staying. The moment a board prices staying, it also prices leaving. That is when alternatives get their first serious hearing, and the end of 2027 is little more than a year away.

3. The road leads to the cloud, on the vendor's terms

The 2031 to 2033 transition option is offered only to customers who move their ECC systems into SAP ERP, private edition, on SAP HANA, before the end of 2030, under a RISE with SAP contract. After 2033, the remaining SAP paths are RISE or GROW. In other words, the support route ends in SAP's subscription cloud.

Cloud brings real benefits in speed and upgrades. But in Oil, Gas and Power, I have argued that the ERP should stay under the owner's control, because data is the new gold and owning your licences and your data keeps the exit in your hands. Some companies, offered a choice between renting the vendor's cloud and building something they own, will now choose to build.

4. AI is writing the alternatives, every day

CIO names AI assisted coding as one of the three reasons IT leaders are looking at replacement. In my view this is the force that changes everything else. A tool that once needed a team of developers for a year can now be built by a small team in weeks. New AI written applications for purchasing, inventory, maintenance and finance are appearing at a speed no ERP release cycle can match.

Agentic AI goes further. An agent can raise a requisition, check it against the budget and the contract, route it for approval and post it, working across systems. When agents do the work, the ERP stops being the place where work happens and becomes a database underneath. A database is much easier to replace than a way of working.

5. ERP was sold to companies that never needed all of it

38,000+organisations ran SAP applications in 2006 (SAP Annual Report 2006)
440,000+business and public customers today (SAP)

SAP's customer base, 2006 and today

SAP's own figures show its customer base growing more than elevenfold in twenty years, and SAP says about 80 percent of its customers are small and midsize enterprises. Not every one of them runs a full ERP, because the count covers all SAP products. But the direction is clear. Big suites moved down market, into companies that would never have bought them in 2006.

Those companies use a fraction of what they pay for. They are the first who will find that a tailor made, AI built system covers their real needs at a fraction of the cost. SAP knows this ground matters: on its full year 2025 results call, its CEO said the mid-market is now by far the fastest growing part of its customer base. That is exactly where the contest will be fought.

6. New builders: specialist firms, SIs and your own IT team

This section and the next are my forecasts, not reported facts.

I expect three kinds of builder to take share from the big suites:

  • Small specialist firms that build tailor made solutions for one industry or one process, priced well below a suite licence.
  • System integrators with their own platforms. SIs have spent decades configuring someone else's product. With AI, many will offer home grown solutions they own, rather than resell a licence they do not.
  • Company IT departments that build and run their own applications, process by process.

In midsize companies running oversized ERPs, this is already happening at a micro level: individual processes are being rebuilt in-house, or the plans are being made. It does not show up in surveys about "replacing the ERP", because nobody calls it that.

7. Fewer coders, many more builders

AI will absorb much of routine coding, and some coding jobs will go. But every company that builds instead of buys will need people inside its own IT department who own the data, design and test the agents, control access and keep the audit trail. I expect the opportunity inside company IT departments to grow far faster than the coding jobs that disappear. The winners will be people who understand both the business process and the AI.

Why big corporates will move last

Large corporates will take longest. Their systems and standard operating procedures have been built around the ERP for decades, and many have grown heavy, bureaucratic and stale. Every change needs committees, audits and regulators. Gartner's three to six years is a fair estimate for them. But even there, the change will start from below: in business units, joint ventures and midsize subsidiaries that build first and prove it works.

The case for staying, and why it proves the point

The other side deserves a fair hearing. On its full year 2025 results call, SAP's CEO Christian Klein said many customers have found that an LLM alone is not enough, and that they need business data and context to build valuable AI. A finance systems director at Herc Rentals told CIO that licence cost is usually the smallest part of an ERP migration, and that companies must first map their data, redesign their controls and keep their people.

I agree with both. That is my point. The value was never in the ERP licence. It is in clean, governed master data and the controls built around it. Whoever owns clean data owns the exit. Whoever does not is locked in, whatever the contract says.

Monday morning

  1. Confirm your ECC enhancement package and your real support date.
  2. Map the modules you pay for against the modules you actually use.
  3. Start the Material Master and Equipment Master clean up, and own the data.
  4. Do not sign a RISE contract under deadline pressure. Get data export and exit terms in writing first.
  5. Pilot one AI built replacement on a non critical process, with proper controls.
  6. Grow an AI capable IT team instead of cutting it.
  7. Take independent advice, and ask every adviser to tell you in writing what they earn from your decision.
ERP will not be switched off. It will be hollowed out, one process at a time, until the licence is the only thing left.
